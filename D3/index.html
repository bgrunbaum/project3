How has climate change affected sea surface temperature?

A per-cell linear trend of annual-mean SST, fitted over 1850–2014

Adjust the threshold to highlight areas warming at or above that rate. Cells below it fade out; the percentage is the share of ocean area (weighted by latitude) at or above it.

Scroll or pinch to zoom · drag to pan · double-click or use the ⟲ button to reset

land

Data: MIROC6 historical run (r5i1p1f1), sea-surface temperature (tos), on the model's native grid of about 1°×1° cells.

Transformations: Monthly values are averaged into annual means. Land cells are excluded, and the map is cropped to 80°S–63°N, where the model grid lines up with latitude. For each end year, a straight line is fitted through every annual value from 1850 to that year, and its slope is reported in °C per decade. "Total change" multiplies that trend by the length of the window. The global mean and the "% of ocean area" readout weight each cell by cos(latitude), so the smaller high-latitude cells don't count too much. The color scale is fixed across years and clamped at ±0.1 °C/decade (±1 °C for total change).

Write-Up

We chose to visualize sea surface temperature since we thought it was a particularly prescient indicator of how climate change is affecting our world, as sea surface temperature has direct implications for El Niño and other meteorological phenomena that can increase warm temperatures and storm frequency. As we looked at the data and did exploratory static visualizations, we found that the most effective method of displaying our temperature data would be on a map showing the trend in temperature change, as it can more effectively show the changes in temperature to the user and where it is changing most.

The sea surface temperature data was measured per latitude and longitude (so that each cell observed on the map is one degree wide and long), and for each cell, the sea surface temperature was taken monthly. To better communicate our data, we compacted those monthly values into an annual mean for each cell, and then for each end year (by default, 2014, but it can be altered), a straight line is fitted through every annual value from 1850 to that year, and its slope is reported in °C per decade. This slope is essentially the rate at which that cell's sea surface temperature has changed over the past x number of years. "Total change" multiplies that trend by how many years are in the total timespan.

In terms of our visual encodings and the colors we chose, we hoped to make our visualization as intuitive as possible for the user to understand. Therefore, we chose a gradient scale of dark red to dark blue to represent extremely warm changes and extremely cold (respectively) changes in temperature (given in degrees Celsius per decade), with stagnant/limited changes being represented by white and lighter values of red and blue. We understand that the map still appears a bit pixelated; unfortunately, that is due to the nature of the latitude and longitude data but we are exploring how to potentially use existing map data (such as from MapWorks) to better unify the map in our Final Project.

Finally, we wanted to consider how we can best make this visualization interactive. When we were making our static visualizations, such as one where we looked at the overall change in sea surface temperature, we found it insightful but it touched on a very broad area (from 1850-2014). We wanted to give viewers the ability to look at a certain time era (as accomplished by the slider), to be able to zoom and inspect an individual cell (as done by the tooltip and zoom functions), and to also be able to filter to look at what areas have been warmed by a certain degree (as done with the input filter box).
